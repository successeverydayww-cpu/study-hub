How to construct a regular hexagon

Construct a regular hexagon of a given side, plus pentagon and octagon.

🎥 Watch and learn (English only)

▶ Constructing a Hexagon (with a compass) — vinteachesmath
Open on YouTube
▶ How to Construct a Regular Hexagon — Example
Open on YouTube
▶ Construct a Regular Hexagon from a Circle (Kofi Dwirah)
Open on YouTube
▶ How to Draw a Hexagon of a Given Side — Blueprint Basics
Open on YouTube

✍ Model answer — write this

Write this: A regular hexagon is constructed inside a circle whose radius equals the side of the hexagon. With centre O and radius = side, draw the circle. Keeping the compass at the same radius, step six arcs round the circumference from point to point. Join the six points: this is the hexagon (all sides equal, all interior angles 120°). Check values: across corners = 2 × side, across flats = 1.732 × side. Pentagon: 360° ÷ 5 = 72° per side; octagon: 45°. General rule for any regular polygon: central angle = 360° ÷ n.

📝 Step-by-step method

  1. Draw a circle with radius = side of the hexagon.
  2. Keep the same compass radius, step 6 arcs around the circle.
  3. Join the six points in order. Check all sides equal.
  4. Across corners = 2 × side. Across flats = 1.732 × side.
  5. Pentagon: 72° per side. Octagon: 45°. Rule: 360° ÷ n.
Exam tip: Hexagon side = circle radius. This one fact builds the hexagonal symbol, the bolt head and the nut.

Bolt and nut thread terms

Draw a bolt and nut showing: external thread, internal thread, axis, major diameter, minor diameter, pitch diameter, pitch, crest, root, depth of thread and angle of thread.

🎥 Watch and learn (English only)

▶ Simplified Representation of Hex-Headed Cap Screw (Engineering Drafting)
Open on YouTube

✍ Model answer — write this

Write this out (one line per term, each with a leader line on your drawing):
1. External thread — the thread cut on the outside of the bolt shank.
2. Internal thread — the thread cut on the inside of the nut hole.
3. Axis — the imaginary centre line running through the bolt and nut.
4. Major diameter — the largest diameter of the thread, measured across the crests (external) or roots (internal).
5. Minor diameter — the smallest diameter, measured across the roots (external).
6. Pitch diameter — the diameter of the imaginary cylinder passing where the thread width equals the space width; it lies between major and minor.
7. Pitch — the distance from a point on one thread to the corresponding point on the next, measured parallel to the axis.
8. Crest — the top surface joining the two flanks of a thread.
9. Root — the bottom surface joining the two flanks.
10. Depth of thread — the perpendicular distance between crest and root = (major − minor) ÷ 2.
11. Angle of thread — the angle between the two flanks; 60° for the ISO metric V-thread.
Draw the bolt shank with a V-thread profile, the nut in section beside it, and label every term exactly as in your Figure 1E-1.

📝 Step-by-step method

  1. Your Figure 1E-1 shows every term the examiner wants. Reproduce it with all labels.
  2. Draw the bolt with the V-thread profile and the nut in section.
  3. Label each of the 11 terms with a leader line. One mark per label.
  4. Add title block and neat lettering.
Exam tip: Figure 1E-1 is your answer key. Every label with a leader line is a scored mark.

Truncated pentagonal prism development

A pentagonal prism, base edge 25 mm, axis 50 mm, rests on its base with one base edge at 45° to the V.P. A section plane V.T. inclined at 30° to the H.P. passes through a point 25 mm from the base along the axis. Develop the lateral surface of the truncated prism.

🎥 Watch and learn (English only)

▶ Sections of Solids — Prism (Engineering Graphics, M Senthil Kumar)
Open on YouTube
▶ Development of Lateral Surface of Pentagonal Prism Cut by AIP
Open on YouTube

✍ Model answer — write this

Values to compute and write:
Perimeter of the development line = 5 × 25 = 125 mm
Circumradius of the pentagon (corner distance from centre) = 25 ÷ (2 sin 36°) = 21.3 mm
Apothem (mid-edge distance) = 25 ÷ (2 tan 36°) = 17.2 mm
Since the plane passes through the axis point at 25 mm at 30°, the cut height on any corner = 25 ± (its horizontal distance from that axis point) × tan 30° (tan 30° = 0.577). The cut heights therefore range from about 25 − 21.3 × 0.577 ≈ 12.7 mm to 25 + 21.3 × 0.577 ≈ 37.3 mm, all within the 50 mm height, so all five edges are cut.
Then write: Draw the top view (pentagon, one edge at 45° to XY), project the front view, mark the cutting plane at 30° through the axis point 25 mm up, find where it meets each of the five vertical edges. Draw the stretch-out line 125 mm long in five 25 mm parts, erect perpendiculars, transfer each true cut height, join the tops with straight lines (the top of a prism development is always straight lines, not a curve), complete the base, label corners A to E.

📝 Step-by-step method

  1. Top view: pentagon, side 25 mm, one edge at 45° to XY. Corners numbered.
  2. Front view: 50 mm tall, five vertical edge lines.
  3. Cutting plane at 30° through the axis point 25 mm up.
  4. Mark the five cut points on the edges.
  5. Stretch-out line 125 mm, five parts of 25 mm, perpendiculars at each.
  6. Transfer true cut heights from the front view, join with straight lines.
  7. Complete the base and label A to E.
Exam tip: Perimeter 125 mm, cut heights between 12.7 and 37.3 mm. The top edge of the development is straight lines, never a curve.

Isometric and orthographic views (Fig II)

Considering Figure II, draw: (i) Isometric drawing, (ii) Front view in direction A, (iii) Side view, (iv) Top view.

🎥 Watch and learn (English only)

▶ Convert Orthographic View to Isometric, step by step
Open on YouTube
▶ Isometric Drawing from Front and Side Views (Prof Jeff)
Open on YouTube

✍ Model answer — write this

Write this: The component has a base block 100 long, a raised step, an inclined bracket at about 45° carrying a circular hole Ø30, and a small block. (ii) Front view (direction A): shows the slope of the bracket and the heights 25, 45 and 60, with the Ø30 circle in its true shape (a circle, because the face is parallel to the V.P. in that view). (iii) Side view: project across, showing the depths 30, 40 and 50. (iv) Top view: project below the front view; widths match the side view (use the 45° mitre line to transfer depths). (i) Isometric drawing: draw the three axes at 30° to horizontal, take true lengths along them (isometric drawing, not isometric projection, uses full 0.816 scale-free lengths), build the base block, then the step, then the inclined bracket and small block; the Ø30 circle becomes an ellipse using the four-centre method inside the isometric face. Hidden lines are omitted in isometric drawings.

📝 Step-by-step method

  1. Orthographic first: front view in direction A (heights 25, 45, 60, circle Ø30).
  2. Side view: depths 30, 40, 50. Top view below the front.
  3. 45° mitre line to transfer depths between top and side.
  4. Isometric: axes at 30°, true lengths, base block then step then bracket.
  5. The circle Ø30 becomes an ellipse by the four-centre method.
Exam tip: Ortho views first, isometric last. The isometric steals its dimensions from the orthographic views.

Development of right cylinders

Develop the lateral surface of a right cylinder cut by an inclined plane (Examples 5, 6, 7), using 12 equal divisions.

🎥 Watch and learn (English only)

▶ Development of Surfaces: Pipe Bend (Prof Vinodkumar)
Open on YouTube

✍ Model answer — write this

Write this: Divide the right cross-section of the cylinder into 12 equal parts (30° apart) and number 0 to 12. Project each division up the elevation as a surface line meeting the cutting plane. Draw the stretch-out line perpendicular to the axis, length = circumference = π × D, divided into the same 12 equal parts. Erect perpendiculars and set off each true length from the elevation. Join the tops with a smooth curve: this curve is a sine curve, the developed cut. For an oblique cylinder (Example 7), the stretch-out line is drawn perpendicular to the axis (the right section), never along the base.

📝 Step-by-step method

  1. 12 divisions of the right cross-section, numbered 0–12.
  2. Surface lines on the elevation meet the cutting plane.
  3. Stretch-out line = πD, 12 equal parts.
  4. Transfer true lengths, join with a smooth sine curve.
  5. Oblique cylinder: stretch-out perpendicular to the axis.
Exam tip: Stretch-out = π × D. The curve is smooth, never made of straight lines.
